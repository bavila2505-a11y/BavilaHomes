Home/Exteriors/Siding

Siding

Repair, partial replacement and full re-side — plus the water barrier and flashing behind it, which is the part that actually keeps the house dry.

First question

Repair or replace

Most people call assuming they need the whole house done. Often they don't, and we'll say so.

Repair makes sense when the damage is localised, the material is still available or matchable, and the water barrier behind it is intact. A failed section under a leaking gutter is a repair, not a re-side.

Replacement makes sense when the failure is general rather than local — fasteners staining across whole elevations, widespread buckling, repeated splitting, or a material at the end of its service life. It also makes sense when we open a wall and find the housewrap is torn, badly lapped, or missing entirely, which is common on anything sided before the mid-1980s.

The honest test: if we'd be back in three years doing the next section, you're better off doing the elevation now.

Behind the siding

The part nobody sees

Siding is a rain screen, not a seal. It's meant to shed most of the water and let the assembly behind it handle the rest. That assembly is where jobs are won or lost.

  • Sheathing — inspected and replaced wherever it's soft. We don't side over compromised sheathing.
  • Water-resistive barrier — housewrap or a self-adhered membrane, lapped shingle-fashion so water always runs out, not in.
  • Flashing — head flashing over every window and door, kickouts where a roof meets a wall, step flashing at rooflines. Missing kickout flashing is the single most common cause of hidden wall rot we find.
  • Drainage plane — a rainscreen gap where the material and exposure call for it, so the back of the siding can dry.
  • Terminations — proper clearance at grade, roof and decking so the siding isn't sitting in water.

A re-side that skips the flashing details is an expensive way to hide the problem for a decade.

Materials

What we install

CedarClapboard or shingle. Beautiful, historically correct for a lot of northern New Jersey housing stock, and the most maintenance. Needs to be back-primed and kept painted or stained.
Fiber cementVery stable, holds paint for a long time, good fire performance. Heavy, and cutting it needs proper dust control. Our usual recommendation for a full re-side where cedar isn't required.
Engineered woodLighter than fiber cement, easier to work, looks closer to real wood. More sensitive to cut edges and ground contact — the details have to be right.
VinylLowest cost and lowest maintenance. Modern insulated products are far better than what's on most houses. Can't be repainted, and colour matching an old repair is usually impossible.
MatchingFor repairs we source the original profile where it still exists, and where it doesn't we'll blend on a natural break line rather than leaving a patch mid-wall.

Process

How a re-side runs

01

Assessment and test opening

We walk every elevation and, with your permission, make a small opening in a suspect area so we can see the sheathing and barrier before quoting rather than guessing at it.

02

Scope and allowance

You get a fixed price for the known work plus a clearly stated unit rate for sheathing replacement, since nobody can know how much is bad until it's open. No open-ended surprises.

03

Tear-off, one elevation at a time

We don't strip the whole house at once. Each elevation gets dried in the same day it's opened, so the house is never left exposed overnight.

04

Repair and dry-in

Bad sheathing out, framing repaired where needed, barrier installed and lapped, all flashing detailed. You get photos of this stage before it's covered.

05

Siding and trim

Installed to the manufacturer's fastening and clearance spec, because that's what the warranty depends on. Trim and terminations detailed as we go.

06

Walkthrough

We walk it with you, top to bottom, before final payment. Anything you're not happy with gets fixed then, not on a punch list you have to chase.
