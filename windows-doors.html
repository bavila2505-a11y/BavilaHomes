Windows
and doors

Replacement, new openings, and the rot that's usually waiting behind the old ones. We do this as carpentry rather than as a sales call — which mostly means we open the wall before quoting, and we flash the opening instead of caulking it.

The detail that matters

Flashed, or caulked

Nearly every rotted window opening we open up failed the same way. Not because the window was cheap — because water was stopped with sealant instead of being given somewhere to go.

Caulk is a consumable. It has a service life of a few years, it fails invisibly, and when it does, the water that gets past it has nowhere to drain and no way out. A flashed opening assumes water will get in and moves it back outside.

Same window. The difference is entirely behind the trim.

Two ways to do it

Insert or full-frame

Every window job is one of these, and the difference is the single biggest factor in what it costs — and in whether you find out what's behind the trim.

Insert replacement

The new window goes inside the existing frame, which stays in the wall. Faster, cheaper, less disruption inside. You lose a little glass area to the extra frame, and — critically — nobody ever sees the rough opening, the sill, or the framing behind it.

Fine when the existing frame is sound and dry. We'll tell you when it is.

Full-frame replacement

The old frame comes out entirely, back to the rough opening. Sill and studs get inspected and replaced where they're gone, a sill pan goes in, the opening gets flashed and integrated with the water barrier, then the new unit is set.

More work and more money. It's also the only version where the failure that killed the last window gets corrected instead of covered.

If a company quotes a window without opening anything, they're quoting the window. Nobody can price what's behind the trim from the driveway.

How it goes

What actually happens

01

We look, and we probe

Every opening gets checked, not just the one that's obviously bad. A moisture meter and an awl tell us more than a visual. You get an assessment showing which openings need full-frame work and which don't.

02

Order, then schedule

Windows are made to your openings and take weeks to arrive. We measure, order, and only schedule the work once units are physically in hand — so your house never sits open waiting on a delivery.

03

Open and assess

Old unit out, opening exposed. This is where we find out what the wall has been doing. You get photographs of every opening before anything goes back — including the ones that turned out fine.

04

Repair, then flash

Rotted sill, studs or sheathing replaced. Sill pan installed with a back dam and sloped to drain, jambs and head flashed, everything lapped into the water barrier so water runs down and out rather than in.

05

Set, insulate, trim

Unit set plumb and square, shimmed at the load points, low-expansion foam at the perimeter. Interior casing and exterior trim to match what's there — or to correct it if what's there is what caused the problem.

Also doors

Entry, patio and new openings

Entry doorsRot at the jamb bottoms and a threshold that's been shimmed rather than replaced is the usual condition. Sill pan under the new threshold, jambs flashed, and the framing behind checked before the unit goes in.
Patio & slidingWide openings with a long sill mean a lot of water crossing one line. These get pan flashing without exception, and the header above gets verified — sliders are frequently installed under headers sized for a window.
New openingsCutting a new door or widening an existing one is framing work: temporary shoring, a properly sized header, king and jack studs, then the opening built and flashed from scratch.
EgressBedrooms and finished basements have minimum opening requirements for escape. If we're replacing windows in a finished basement or a converted attic, we check this — it's a common violation people inherit unknowingly.
Storm damageImpact and blown-in units, usually urgent. We can secure an opening the same day and do the permanent repair once a unit arrives.
