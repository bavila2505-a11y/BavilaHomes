Outdoor living
structures

Pergolas, gazebos, cabanas, covered porches and outdoor kitchens. These get sold as landscaping, but they're buildings — they need footings below frost depth, real framing, and a roof that sheds water where you want it to. We build them the way we'd build an addition.

What we build

Five things people mean by "backyard"

Most of these projects start as one idea and grow into another. Someone asks about a pergola and ends up with a covered porch, because what they actually wanted was to sit outside in the rain.

It's worth being clear about what each one is, because the cost difference between them is substantial and it comes down to whether there's a roof over your head and what that roof has to carry in a February snow load.

Pergola · one to two weeks

Posts, beams and open rafters. No roof, so no snow load to engineer for — but the posts still need footings below frost depth or the whole thing racks and lifts over a few winters. The failure we get called to fix is a pergola set on surface blocks.

Covered structure · two to five weeks

Gazebo, pavilion or covered porch. Once there's a roof, it carries snow — which in northern New Jersey means it gets engineered and permitted like any other structure. If it attaches to the house, the ledger and its flashing become the most important detail on the job.

Outdoor kitchen · two to six weeks

Framed masonry or steel-stud counters, faced in stone or stucco, with gas, water and power run out and protected. The part people underestimate is winterization — a water line that isn't pitched to drain will split in the first hard freeze.

Cabana or pool house · six to fourteen weeks

An actual building: foundation, framing, roof, siding, and usually electrical and plumbing. Changing rooms, a bathroom, storage. Permitting and setback requirements are the same as any accessory structure, and pool proximity brings its own code requirements.

Where these go wrong

Built like furniture,
not like buildings

Most of the outdoor structures we're called to repair were installed by landscapers or bought as kits. The carpentry isn't usually the problem. What's underneath it is.

  • Surface blocks instead of footings. The ground here heaves. A structure on precast blocks moves every winter until the joints open and the posts go out of plumb.
  • Posts in direct ground contact. Even pressure-treated, a post buried in soil rots from the outside in. Standoff bases on a poured pier keep the end grain out of the water.
  • Ledgers nailed, not bolted. Same failure as a deck, with the same consequences. If it attaches to the house it needs through-bolts or structural screws and flashing above.
  • No permit on a roofed structure. Discovered at resale, when the town has no record of it. Expensive to resolve retroactively and occasionally means taking it down.
  • Gas and water run without protection. Lines that can't be drained, or that run where they'll be hit by a shovel.

A pergola is not landscaping. It's a structure with posts, beams and a footing schedule — and it lasts exactly as long as the worst decision made below grade.

How it's built

What actually happens

01

Layout and permits

Setbacks, easements and pool clearances checked before anything is dug. Roofed structures get a stamped drawing and a permit; open pergolas sometimes don't, depending on the town. We handle the filing.

02

Footings

Sonotubes dug below frost depth, poured, and set with galvanised standoff bases so no post touches soil. Inspected before backfill where the town requires it. This is the step nobody sees and everything depends on.

03

Frame

Posts, beams, rafters. If it ties into the house, the ledger goes on with through-bolts and gets flashed before any decking or roofing covers it. Photographs taken at this stage, before it's closed up.

04

Roof and services

Roofing to match the house where it should match. Gas, water and electrical run by licensed subs, with water lines pitched to drain and shutoffs somewhere you can actually reach in November.

05

Finish

Trim, ceiling, stain or paint, counters and facing. Then a walkthrough covering how to winterize it — which valve, which order, what to do before the first freeze.

Specifics

Materials and detail

FootingsPoured concrete piers below frost depth, typically 36" in northern New Jersey. Galvanised or stainless standoff post bases — no wood in ground contact.
FramingPressure-treated for structure. Western red cedar, mahogany or cellular PVC where it's seen. Stainless or hot-dip galvanised fasteners throughout — electroplated screws stain and fail outdoors.
RoofingMatched to the house where the structure is visible from it. Standing seam, architectural shingle, or polycarbonate where you want light through.
AttachmentThrough-bolted ledgers with proper flashing and a drainage gap. Free-standing wherever attaching would compromise the house.
KitchensGalvanised steel stud or masonry carcass, never wood. Stone, stucco or tile facing. Gas by a licensed plumber, power by a licensed electrician, both permitted.
WinterizationWater lines pitched to drain with accessible shutoffs. Documented at handover so you know what to do each fall.
