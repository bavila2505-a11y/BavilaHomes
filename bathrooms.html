Home/Renovations/Bathrooms

Bathrooms

Full gut renovations, done from the subfloor up. Bathrooms fail because of what's behind the tile, so that's where we spend the effort.

Why bathrooms fail

Tile is not waterproof

This surprises people. Tile and grout both let water through — slowly, but continuously. What keeps a bathroom dry is the waterproofing layer behind the tile, and in most houses built before the 2000s there effectively isn't one.

The old standard was drywall or, worse, regular gypsum board behind the tile in a shower. It works until the grout develops hairline cracks, which it always does. Then water reaches the board, the board fails, and the damage moves into the framing and the ceiling below.

By the time you see a loose tile or a stain on the ceiling downstairs, the assembly behind it has usually been wet for years. That's why we don't do partial shower repairs — patching over a failed substrate just moves the problem.

If your shower has loose tiles, the tiles are not the problem.

Assembly

How we build a wet area

SubstrateCement board or a foam backer board in wet areas. Never paper-faced drywall behind tile in a shower, regardless of what was there before.
WaterproofingA continuous membrane system — sheet or liquid-applied — over the substrate, with the corners, changes of plane and penetrations detailed properly. The corners are where systems fail.
Pan & drainSloped correctly to the drain, with a bonded or clamping drain matched to the waterproofing system. Flood tested before tile goes on.
SubfloorChecked for deflection and repaired or stiffened where needed. Tile is unforgiving of movement, and this is why floors crack.
JoistsInspected while open. Old bathrooms are frequently the place where joists were notched or drilled for plumbing until there was very little joist left.
VentilationA fan sized for the room and ducted to the outside, not into the attic. Interior moisture problems are usually ventilation problems.

Sequence

How a bathroom runs

01

Design and selections

Layout, fixtures, tile and finishes decided before demolition. Tile and vanity lead times are the usual cause of a stalled bathroom.

02

Demolition

Stripped back to framing. This is when we can properly assess the joists, subfloor and any existing damage.

03

Structure and subfloor

Joist repair, subfloor replacement and stiffening where the deflection calls for it.

04

Rough-in and inspection

Plumbing and electrical set for the new layout, vent fan ducted, then inspected before anything is closed.

05

Substrate and waterproofing

Backer board, membrane, pan, and a flood test on the pan before tile. We photograph this stage — it's the part you'll never see again.

06

Tile

Floor and walls, with movement joints where they belong.

07

Fixtures and finish

Vanity, toilet, shower door, trim, paint and hardware. Then the walkthrough.

Typical range: 3–5 weeks for a standard full gut

Worth considering while it's open

  • Blocking for grab bars — cheap now, expensive later, and worth it in any bathroom you plan to age in.
  • A curbless or low-curb shower — needs the subfloor recessed, so it's only practical while the floor is open.
  • Relocating the layout — moving a toilet or shower is far less expensive when the floor is already up.
  • Replacing old supply and drain lines — particularly cast iron or galvanised, which are at the end of their lives in a lot of houses here.
  • Sound insulation — in the walls and floor. Costs very little at this stage and is noticeable forever.
