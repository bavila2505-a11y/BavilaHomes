Home/Framing/Additions

Additions

More house, tied into the house you already have. Bump-outs, full wings, second stories and in-law suites — foundation through finish, under one contract.

Options

Four ways to add space

Bump-outA few feet off an existing room, often cantilevered so it doesn't need a full foundation. The cheapest square footage you can add, and enough to transform a cramped kitchen or bathroom.
Full wingA new room or rooms on a new foundation. The most common addition we build — family rooms, primary suites, expanded kitchens. Needs its own footings, roof and tie-in.
Second storyAdding above rather than out, when the lot won't allow spreading. The existing structure and foundation have to be verified to carry it, which is an engineering question before it's a carpentry one.
In-law / accessory suiteA wing or conversion with its own bath and kitchenette. Zoning rules on these vary sharply by municipality in New Jersey — worth checking feasibility before designing anything.

Before you design

Zoning decides more than you'd think

In most northern New Jersey towns, what you can build is constrained less by engineering than by the zoning ordinance, and the constraints are unusually tight on the older, smaller lots common here.

  • Setbacks — how close you can build to each property line. On a narrow lot this often rules out a side addition entirely.
  • Lot coverage — the maximum percentage of the lot that can be covered by building, and sometimes a separate cap including driveway and patio.
  • Floor area ratio — a cap on total floor area relative to lot size, used by many towns here specifically to limit large additions.
  • Height — the binding constraint on most second-story projects.
  • Non-conforming structures — if the existing house already violates a current setback, expanding it can require a variance even when the addition itself complies.

We check this at the start, before anyone spends money on drawings. If a variance is needed we'll tell you honestly what that process looks like in your town and roughly what it adds in time.

The cheapest addition is the one you find out is impossible before you pay for architectural plans.

Process

How an addition runs

01

Feasibility

We walk the property, pull the zoning requirements for your town, and tell you what's actually buildable. Free, and sometimes the answer is 'not what you were picturing' — better to hear it now.

02

Design and drawings

Architectural drawings, and structural engineering where the project needs it. We work with architects we know, or with yours.

03

Permitting

Building, plus electrical, plumbing and fire sub-permits. Timelines vary a lot by municipality — some towns are two weeks, some are three months. We'll give you a realistic read for yours.

04

Foundation

Excavation, footings below frost depth, foundation walls, drainage and waterproofing. Inspected before backfill.

05

Framing and dry-in

Floor system, walls, roof, and the tie-in to the existing structure. Then sheathing, roofing, windows and housewrap so the addition is weathertight before anything else starts.

06

Mechanicals and inspections

Electrical, plumbing and HVAC rough-in, insulation, and the inspections at each stage. Nothing gets closed up before it's signed off.

07

Finish

Drywall, trim, flooring, paint, and the transition detailing where new meets old — which is the part that decides whether the addition reads as original or as an addition.

08

Final inspection and walkthrough

Certificate of occupancy or approval, then a full walkthrough with you before final payment.

Expectations

Timeline and what goes wrong

A typical single-story wing runs roughly two to four months of construction once permits are issued. Second stories run longer. Permitting itself can add anywhere from a few weeks to several months depending on the town and whether a variance is involved.

The honest risks, stated up front:

  • The tie-in. Existing houses are rarely square, level or built the way the drawings assume. Matching a roofline into an existing one without creating a valley that traps water is genuinely the hard part of most additions.
  • What's already there. Opening the existing wall sometimes reveals undersized framing, old damage or previous work that has to be corrected before the new structure can tie in.
  • Utilities. Older services frequently need upgrading to carry the added load. Worth budgeting for.
  • Matching. Siding, roofing and trim that match a 60-year-old house may no longer be manufactured. We'll discuss whether to match, blend on a break line, or re-side the affected elevation.
