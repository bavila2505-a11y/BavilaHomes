Dormers & garages

Roofline changes that turn unused attic into real rooms, and garages built, rebuilt or converted. Both are framing projects first and everything else second.

Dormers

The cheapest square footage in the house

If you have a full attic with reasonable ridge height, a dormer is usually far less expensive per square foot than an addition — the foundation and most of the roof already exist.

Shed dormerA flat-ish roof running out from the main roof. Gains the most headroom and floor area for the money, which is why it's the standard answer for turning a Cape attic into two bedrooms. Less character from the street.
Gable dormerA small peaked dormer, usually one window wide. Adds much less space but far more to how the house looks. Often used in pairs or threes on the front elevation.
Full shed / rear dormerRunning the dormer nearly the full width at the back. Effectively converts a half-story into a full one. The biggest gain and the biggest job.
What decides itExisting ridge height, roof pitch, where the stairs land, and whether the floor joists below can carry living load rather than just attic storage. That last one catches a lot of people out.

Dormers also need egress-compliant windows in any room used as a bedroom, and a stair that meets code — which sometimes means rebuilding the attic stair as part of the project.

Garages

New, rebuilt or converted

Garages in this part of New Jersey tend to be old, undersized for modern cars, and sitting on foundations that have moved.

  • New detached garages — slab, framing, roof and finish. Constrained by the same setback and lot coverage rules as an addition, so feasibility comes first.
  • Rebuilds — when the existing structure has racked, rotted at the base, or the slab has failed. Often cheaper than it sounds and dramatically better than patching.
  • Attached garage repair — rot at the base of the walls from years of road salt and snowmelt is extremely common, and it's structural work.
  • Conversions — turning a garage into living space. Needs insulation, a proper floor system above the slab, egress, heating, and usually a zoning check on parking requirements.
  • Second-story additions above garages — attractive, but the existing foundation and framing have to be verified before anything else is considered.

Any roofline change is a water-management decision before it's an aesthetic one.

What both have in common

Dormers and garages both cut into or add to the roof and drainage of the house, and both are permitted work with inspections at framing.

The failure mode is the same in both cases: a new roof plane meeting an old one, badly detailed, creating a spot where water collects instead of runs. Flashing, valley detailing and kickouts at the wall are the difference between a dormer that's fine in twenty years and one that quietly rots the wall beneath it.

We frame these, dry them in, and detail the water management ourselves rather than handing that part to whoever shows up next.
